\paragraph{From a population of programs to a useful choice.}
The same-code reference separates sampling opportunities from program
identity, while repeats reveal the direction of persistent differences.
In the tested panel, that persistent structure is concentrated in losses.
Strong repeatability therefore describes where programs fail more
clearly than where a selector can exploit an advantage. This explains
why repeatable score patterns can coexist with zero selection gain and
equal full-budget coverage. The design objective is a task advantage
that persists and can be recognized before execution. BIRD locates
three breaks on the path to such an advantage: a proposed operation
can be absent, remain idle, or execute and return an invalid answer.

\paragraph{Scope.}
The MATH study uses a low-call panel, one solver, and three repeats.
The repeat diagnostic and complete-case reanalysis were developed
after collection; the recovery stage preserved programs, tasks, solver,
and repeat count. The $\RcvExcludedN$ excluded tasks are harder than the
$\RcvPairedN$ included tasks (Appendix~\ref{app:wp1r}). The held-out comparison leaves stable complementarity unresolved
at three repeats, and recorded score patterns include answer-format
effects. Selector results concern
the frozen policy; replay matches harness executions, with model calls
and tokens varying by program. BIRD uses its own populations and
scoring conditions.
