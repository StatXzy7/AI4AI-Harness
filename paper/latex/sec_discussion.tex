\paragraph{From a population of programs to a useful choice.}
The three measurements describe successive parts of the same story.
Coverage records the answers a population makes available. Repeats
reveal which differences persist. Selection tests whether those
differences help a decision made before execution. In the generated
panel, the strongest repeatable structure is a map of losses: persistent
losses span $\RcvLossTasks$ tasks, while persistent wins occur on one.
The frozen selector provides a direct practical result,
$\RcvPolicyGain$ pp gain, alongside the equal full-budget oracle coverage.
For harness design, the relevant target is a repeatable task advantage
that a selection rule can recognize. BIRD adds an execution-level view:
implementation, activation, and a valid final output are successive
requirements for a proposed mechanism to affect behavior.

\paragraph{Scope.}
The MATH study uses a low-call panel, one solver, and three repeats.
The repeat diagnostic and complete-case reanalysis were developed
after collection; the recovery stage preserved programs, tasks, solver,
and repeat count. The $\RcvExcludedN$ excluded tasks are harder than the
$\RcvPairedN$ included tasks (Appendix~\ref{app:wp1r}). The held-out comparison leaves stable complementarity unresolved
at three repeats, and recorded score patterns include answer-format
effects. Selector results concern
the frozen policy; replay matches harness executions, with model calls
and tokens varying by program. BIRD uses its own populations and
scoring conditions.
