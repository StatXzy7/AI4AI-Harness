\paragraph{Implications for evaluation.}
Our results separate three observations that can otherwise be treated
as interchangeable evidence. Identical programs produce positive oracle
headroom; generated programs produce repeatable score patterns; the
frozen selector nevertheless realizes no gain, and full-budget oracle
coverage ties. A claim of useful specialization must therefore connect
repeatable \emph{advantages}, task-wise crossover, and a usable selection
rule. In the generated panel, persistent losses span $\RcvLossTasks$
tasks, while persistent wins occur on one: the reproducible structure
is largely a map of where programs lose, rather than where a selector
can gain.
If an advantage is attributed to a mechanism, traces must establish
what ran and an intervention must test its contribution.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The controlled MATH panel is a low-call draw under one solver on
previously evaluated tasks. The revised held-out-repeat procedure and
added repeatability analysis were developed after collection. The
initial study ended under its stopping rule; the separately recorded
recovery stage followed inspection of those earlier results, with
programs, tasks, solver, and repeat count held fixed.
The $\RcvPairedN$-task analysis set is fully
observed and selected by completeness. The $\RcvExcludedN$ excluded
problems have higher observed difficulty and lower baseline accuracy
(Appendix~\ref{app:wp1r}); performance estimates therefore describe
the selected cohort. Three repeats have low diagnostic power, and
stable score patterns can include formatting effects from the fixed
judge. The calibrated result remains \textsc{Insufficient Evidence}.
The zero routing gain concerns the tested selector and features.
Replay standardizes harness execution counts; model-call counts and
token costs vary by program. BIRD findings apply to its own judging
and acquisition conditions.
