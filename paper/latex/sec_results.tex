\subsection{Positive Oracle Estimates from Identical Programs}
\label{sec:f3-clones}
The same-code arm provides the most direct check of the specialization
interpretation: all nine slots implement the same program, so stable
program complementarity is zero. If its estimated headroom is positive,
that estimate cannot by itself distinguish specialization from
execution variability.

The repeat-averaged estimate is indeed positive:
$\widehat H=\WpVthreeClonePlugin$\,\% on $383$ complete clone tasks.
The generated panel gives $\WpVthreePlugin$\,\% on its $350$ complete
tasks (Table~\ref{tab:clone-control}). These are arm-specific descriptive
estimates, not a paired effect comparison. Unknowns remain missing;
the older available-repeat estimates use a different estimand and
are documented in Appendix~\ref{app:wp1r}. The control establishes
that even an oracle estimate averaged over three repeats can be
positive without program specialization. The next question is whether
choices discovered in some repeats remain useful in another.

\subsection{Repeat Validation and Diagnostic Sensitivity}
\label{sec:repeat-result}
Freezing choices before scoring reduces reuse of chance successes.
On the respective complete-task sets, the held-out-repeat mean is
$G_{\rm real}=\WpVthreeG$\,\% for generated programs and
$G_{\rm clone}=\WpVthreeGclone$\,\% for clones. On their
$\WpVthreeNPaired$-task intersection, the calibrated difference is
$D=\WpVthreeD$\,\%, with 95\% interval $\WpVthreeDCI$\,\%.
The mean shows no advantage over the same-code reference. It also
establishes neither equivalence nor a negative population effect:
$\WpExclReal\%$ of generated-arm tasks are incomplete, exceeding the
$10\%$ gate. The decision is \textsc{Insufficient Evidence}.
All-$0$/all-$1$ filling scenarios give $G$ values spanning
$\WpVthreeBounds$\,\%; the full decision rule and sensitivity methods appear
in Appendix~\ref{app:statistics}, with missingness records in
Appendix~\ref{app:wp1r}.

\paragraph{Would the diagnostic detect real crossovers?}
\label{sec:power}
Known-truth simulations answer this necessary follow-up. Across a
$100$-cell grid varying members, tasks, repeats, and missingness, the
clone-calibrated rule's worst observed null false-support rate is
$2\%$. Sensitivity is much less reassuring: a two-specialist setting
with true $H_{\rm stable}=17.5$\,\% has $0\%$ power at $R=3$, about
$0.7\%$ at $R=5$, and $100\%$ at $R=10,20$. Smaller cyclic crossovers
remain largely undetected even at $R=20$
(Appendix Table~\ref{tab:calibration}). These are results for the tested
simulation settings, not a universal repeat threshold.

The two findings support different conclusions. The clone result
refutes using a positive plugin alone to identify specialization.
The repeat result does not settle whether this generated panel has
stable crossovers: the data are incomplete and the test can miss
known crossovers at three repeats. Some stable mean member differences
are detected, but a stronger average member is not itself a task
specialist. Although stable specialization remains unresolved, selection performance
can still be measured directly. We next use the same MATH panel to test
a frozen pre-execution choice and compare post-execution coverage at
equal execution counts.
