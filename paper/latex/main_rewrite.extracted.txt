                                                 Abstract
010
011
                An automatically generated harness can differ from its peers in source code,
012             execution trace, and correctness outcomes. These differences are often
013             treated as evidence of complementary capability, although each can also
014             arise from an inactive branch, an invalid implementation, or stochastic
015             execution. We study this measurement problem for harnesses wrapping
016             a frozen language model on BIRD text-to-SQL. A discovery population
017             contained 36 outcome-identical pairs among 91 pairs despite source-code
018             differences; trace audits separated absent, untriggered, and broken mech-
019             anisms. We then reanalyze a frozen 2 × 2 study crossing strategy forcing
020
                with conformance gating over six builders and three seeds. On 1,169 iso-
                lated questions, free ungated generation already had 8.56 ppbare-inclusive
021
                oracle headroom. The archived forced-and-gated versus free-and-ungated
022
                contrast was −0.16 pp (95% CI [−0.83, +0.87]), with no clear improvement.
023             The gate also excludes two allowed prompt-level strategies, so this is a com-
024             bined screening and conformance contrast rather than a pure verification
025             effect. Repeated executions further show that one-run oracle headroom
026             can be created by the same code. We therefore distinguish source diversity,
027             executed mechanisms, one-run outcome diversity, stable complementarity,
028             and realized selection utility. The current evidence motivates an auditable
029             protocol for measuring these quantities, but does not establish stable addi-
030             tional capability.

      1     Introduction
033
034   Large language models are increasingly deployed inside harnesses: programs that orga-
035   nize prompts, tool calls, state, verification, and output formatting around a frozen model.
036   Harness-generation systems can produce many candidate programs, creating a natural
037   population-selection problem. A common analysis asks whether the candidates have differ-
038   ent source code or whether their correctness vectors differ on a test set. Neither observation
039   answers the practical question that matters for deployment:
040
                Does the population contain additional capability that survives independent
041
                executions and can be selected at a fixed cost?
042
043   The distinction is easy to lose. A branch may be described but never executed; a repair
044   branch may exist but never be triggered; two valid mechanisms may produce the same
045   answer on the sampled tasks; or one stochastic execution of a single harness may look
046   like several complementary harnesses. In each case, source-level diversity can overstate
047   behavioral diversity, and one-run behavioral diversity can overstate stable complementarity.
048   We examine these failure modes on BIRD text-to-SQL (Li et al., 2023). The paper has
049   two stages. Phase I is a discovery study that documents the measurement problem in an
050   early generated population. Phase II is a frozen factorial study that asks whether strategy
051   forcing and conformance gating change the observed bare-inclusive oracle headroom under
052   a common generation budget. We report a post-review reanalysis of the archived Phase-II
053   outcomes. This reanalysis corrects the resampling and data-loading chain; it does not add
      independent executions or repair the original gate.

054
      Our contributions are deliberately narrower than a claim that generated harnesses improve
055   deployment performance:
056
057        1. We define separate measurements for source, trace, outcome, and stable complemen-
058           tarity, and give a counterexample showing why oracle headroom is not monotone
059           in population size.
060        2. We provide a trace-based audit taxonomy and a factorial analysis that makes the
061           implemented admission policy explicit, including its structural exclusion of two
062
              allowed prompt strategies.
           3. We report the evidence boundary exposed by repeated execution, fixed-K sensitiv-
063
              ity, and post-hoc selection analyses. These controls show why a positive one-run
064
              headroom value is a necessary diagnostic for routing research, but not evidence of
065           a usable selector.
066
067   The central unresolved test is a cost-matched comparison between a population of genuinely
068   different harnesses and a clone population formed by independent executions of the same
069   code. The present data do not contain that control; we keep stable complementarity as an
070   estimand rather than presenting it as a finding.
071
072   2   Related Work
073
074   Recent work generates, evolves, or routes harnesses around frozen language models, includ-
075   ing harness evolution, self-harnessing, just-in-time harness generation, and task–harness co-
076   evolution (Nie et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026b;a; Miyai et al., 2026). Concurrent behavior-
077   aware systems use traces or semantic screening inside their search loops (Xu et al., 2026;
078   Chen et al., 2026; Lin et al., 2026; Luo et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026). Our question is
079   different: what properties does the resulting population have as an outcome matrix, and
080   which generation or admission policy changes those properties?
081   The distinction matters because a behavior-aware gate inside an evolution loop does not
082   by itself establish population complementarity. Likewise, portfolio routing and algorithm-
083   selection work assumes that candidate systems provide reliable conditional value (Rice,
084   1976; Xu et al., 2008; Kotthoff, 2014); our study measures the conditions that a future
085   router would need to inherit. HELIX and related work emphasize sibling-harness outcome
086   matrices and complementarity (Fan & Huang, 2026), while studies of harness sensitivity
087
      show that no single harness need dominate across instances (Gupta et al., 2026; Cho, 2026).
      We build on these ideas by separating one-run coverage from repeat-stable value and by
088
      auditing the instrument that decides whether a mechanism was actually present.

091   3   Measurement Framework
092
093   Let H wrap a frozen target model M , receive an item x, make zero or more model calls
094   and tool executions, and return an answer scored by a judge. Let H0 be the bare single-call
095
      baseline and Y (x, H, M ) ∈ {0, 1} its correctness. The item-level marginal value is
096                            ∆(x, H, M ) = Y (x, H, M ) − Y (x, H0 , M ).
097
098
      For a population P , we report three one-run quantities. Pairwise outcome disagreement is
      the fraction of items on which two members have different correctness. Union repair is the
099
      fraction of bare errors repaired by at least one member. Bare-inclusive oracle headroom is
100                              (                          )

104
      Positive H(P ) means that, in this particular outcome matrix, different members cover dif-
      ferent items beyond the best fixed member. It does not imply that a selector can identify
105
      those members before seeing the answer.

141   4   Phase I: Discovery Audit
142
143   Phase I uses GLM-5.3-Flash at temperature zero on 60 BIRD questions. It is discovery evi-
144   dence: 24 items overlapped protocol development, the human positive control was designed
145   after trace inspection, and the legacy judge was a conservative proxy. These conditions
146
      support diagnosis, not confirmation.
147   The day-1 population contains bare, react, and 12 generated candidates. Across all 14
148   harnesses, 36 of 91 pairs have identical correctness vectors and mean pairwise disagreement
149   is 3.3%. Among the 12 generated candidates alone, 21 of 66 pairs are identical and mean
150   disagreement is 3.8%. The candidate pair code-similarity mean is 0.143 under the original
151   normalized-character metric. In the full population, the token-sequence metric used in
152
      Figure 1 has mean 0.190 and Spearman ρ = −0.010 with outcome disagreement. Pairs
      share harnesses, so this is a descriptive summary rather than an independent sample test.
153
154   Trace inspection identifies three actionable classes. T1 mechanisms are described but absent
155   from control flow: six of 12 candidates make one model call and execute no artifact. T2
156   mechanisms are present but untriggered: in all 10 audited react tasks, the first SQL succeeds,
157   so conditional repair does not run. T3 is a broken multi-turn path: one candidate returns
158
      prose as final SQL and turns some bare-correct items into errors. These classes require
      different controls. A mechanism gate must test execution, a repair claim must be tested
159
      with controlled faults, and a multi-turn harness must validate its executable output.
160
161   Hand-written positive controls show that task-side headroom exists in the observed matrix:
      forcibly distinct execution paths give 6.67 ppon 60 items and 7.28 ppon a 151-item probe.

186   5   Phase II: Frozen Factorial Study
187
188
189   Phase II crosses two factors over six builders and three seeds. The free arm allows the
190   builder to invent a mechanism; the forced arm assigns one of eight named strategies. The
191   ungated arm applies neutral validity checks; the gated arm additionally requires the frozen
192   conformance suite. We call the four arms II-A (free/ungated), II-B (free/gated), II-C
193   (forced/ungated), and II-D (forced/gated). An open-mechanism arm II-E is secondary and
194
      outside the factorial.
195   The conformance suite drives scripted scenarios and checks recorded traces, including paired
196   counterfactuals whose correct answers differ. It was calibrated before Phase-II generation
197   with three positive controls and two negative impostors. The implemented gate maps both
198   hint_guard and format_guard to a no-mechanism result. Therefore II-D versus II-A com-
199   bines strategy forcing, structural screening, and conformance verification; it cannot identify
200
      verification alone.
201   Each slot permits at most three raw builder attempts and stops at the first accepted can-
202   didate. Realized attempts were 269, 255, 273, and 324 for II-A through II-D against a
203   432-attempt arm cap; realized token cost is not fully recovered. Slots are not topped up
204   after failure, so generation reliability is part of the policy and is reported separately from
205   population quality.
206   The target is GLM-5.3-Flash. The primary set has 1,169 questions from nine databases
207   never opened during development, with a disclosed 18-item smoke-test exception. All 18
208   builder–seed cells enter the primary analysis, including empty populations. The oﬀicial
209   BIRD execution scorer is primary; the legacy judge is a conservative secondary check. A
210   400-item core, frozen before outcomes, supports the four-arm secondary analysis.
211   The primary estimand is the paired cell difference H(II-D) − H(II-A) on the full set. We
212   use shared database and within-database task resampling, generation-seed resampling within
213   builder, and a sign-flip reference test conditional on observed tasks. The factorial secondary
214   estimands on the common core are the gate main effect, strategy main effect, and their
215   interaction. These are conditional analyses of archived executions, not randomized tests of
      an abstract gate.

241   6.1   Primary contrast
242
      Table 1 reports the corrected reanalysis. Free ungated generation already produces 76.14%
243
      oracle accuracy, 67.58% best-fixed accuracy, and 8.56 ppheadroom. Forced gated generation
244   produces 75.70% oracle accuracy, 67.30% best-fixed accuracy, and 8.40 ppheadroom. The
245   paired headroom contrast is −0.16 pp with 95% CI [−0.83, +0.87] and one-sided sign-flip
246   p = 0.647; cell contrasts range from −4.36 to + 2.74 pp.
247
      The interval does not establish equivalence, but it provides no clear evidence that the im-
248
      plemented forced-and-gated policy improves headroom. Because the gate excludes allowed
249
      prompt transformations, the result should not be described as a pure conformance effect.

253
      On the common 400-item core, the corrected gate main effect is -0.69 ppwith Holm-
254   adjusted p = 0.079; the strategy effect is +0.42 pp(adjusted p = 0.428) and the interac-

      For the full primary set, oracle accuracy changes by -0.44 ppand best-fixed accuracy by -

      6.3   Execution variability and selection boundary
264
265   Across three cache-off repeats, 14.5% of bare tasks changed correctness at least once.
266   A canonical R2 replay found 2,391 same-harness verdict flips among 28,000 comparisons
267   (8.54%). The cache-off subset contrast changed from +0.42 pp to +0.81 pp; its change was
268   +0.39 pp with 95% CI [−0.95, +1.63]. These are instability measurements, not standard
269   errors or a noise floor for the primary effect. They do not show that every contrast is
      unchanged.

291   7   Discussion
292
293   The results support a measurement conclusion. Code differences, executed mechanisms,
294   one-run correctness differences, repeat-stable complementarity, and realized selector value
295   are different objects. The Phase-I population makes the first boundary visible. The Phase-II
296   archive tests one policy contrast but does not remove the second boundary: observed oracle
297   headroom can contain execution randomness, and the gate’s structural exclusions prevent
298
      a pure verification interpretation.
299   This framing also changes how population size should be interpreted. Increasing the number
300   of candidates can increase oracle coverage, leave it unchanged, or reduce headroom if a strong
301   fixed member improves the best-fixed comparator. Similarly, a K-matched difference tells us
302   how the estimand changes after subsampling; it does not identify a causal share attributable
303   to population size.
304   The most informative next experiment is a cost-matched clone control. It should use the
305   same raw pool, interleave real and clone executions, run each code path under cache-on and
306   cache-off conditions, and reserve independent repeats for validation. It should report abso-
307   lute oracle accuracy, best-fixed accuracy, realized selector accuracy if a selector is specified,
308   candidate count, and cost. A gate calibration set should separately include legal single-call
309   rewrites, conditional repair, unconditional extra calls, discarded feedback, position-based
310
      voting, and untriggered repair. This is necessary to distinguish an absent mechanism, an
      untriggered mechanism, a nonresponsive mechanism, and an implementation failure.
311
312   The evidence is limited to one main target, six fixed builders, and three generation seeds.
313   Phase I contains development overlap and a post-trace human control. The corrected anal-
314   ysis is post-review and does not add executions. The one-seed MATH-500 probe in the
315   supplement extends the outcome comparison to a second domain, but has no clone con-
316
      trol, repaired-gate factorial, or independent stable-complementarity test. Partial provider
      acquisitions are retained for provenance and missingness accounting and are excluded from
317
      population-performance claims.

320   8   Conclusion
321
322   AI-generated harness populations should be audited at the level of what they execute and
323   what remains reliable across repeats. In our archived BIRD study, the implemented forced-
      and-gated policy does not show a clear headroom gain, and the available one-run headroom
