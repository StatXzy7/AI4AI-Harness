A population can supply more correct answers without containing
task specialists, and specialists can exist without an effective
selector. We formalize these distinctions before describing the controls.
Let $P$ contain baseline $H_0$, and let $Y(h,x,r)\in\{0,1\}$ denote
member $h$'s correctness on task $x$ in repeat $r$. Throughout, gains
use \% for absolute probability differences:
$98.50\%-93.75\%=4.75\%$ means $0.0475$ additional success probability.

\paragraph{Coverage: were additional correct answers available?}
A one-run oracle sees correctness before choosing a member. Its
headroom over the best fixed member in the recorded matrix is
\begin{equation}
H(P)=\tfrac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\max_{h\in P}Y(h,x_i,r)
-\max_{h\in P}\tfrac1n\sum_{i=1}^nY(h,x_i,r).
\label{eq:headroom}
\end{equation}
This measures available answers, including successes from repeated
sampling. The repeat-averaged plugin $\widehat H$ replaces $Y$ by each
member--task mean. With few repeats, maximizing those means can still
select favorable estimation errors; $\widehat H>0$ is not by itself
evidence of specialization.

\paragraph{Stable interaction: does the best member change with the task?}
To remove execution noise conceptually, write
$p_h(x)=\Pr\{Y(h,x,r)=1\}$ under a fixed execution condition and define
$H_{\rm stable}=\mathbb E_x\max_h p_h(x)-\max_h\mathbb E_x p_h(x)$.
For two members with $\Delta(x)=p_1(x)-p_0(x)$,
\begin{equation}
H_{\rm stable}=\tfrac12\bigl(\mathbb E|\Delta(x)|-|\mathbb E\Delta(x)|\bigr).
\label{eq:identity}
\end{equation}
If one member is better everywhere, the expression is zero, even when
the size of its advantage varies. Positive complementarity requires
task-wise crossover. Identical programs under the same condition have
equal $p_h(x)$ and hence zero stable complementarity. This population
identity clarifies the target; it does not estimate unknown probabilities
from a small number of executions.

\paragraph{Usable selection: can the advantage inform a choice?}
A selector $\pi_Z$ must choose from task features $Z(x)$ before seeing
the result. Even stable crossovers may not be predictable from those
features. Learning a choice from earlier outcomes on the \emph{same}
task instead tests repeat generalization, a different information
setting. We evaluate both separately. Practical resource allocation
adds one more comparison: a selection gain over one fixed execution
must be assessed against additional fixed-program executions under a
common budget and answer-selection rule. Equal numbers of harness
executions need not entail equal model calls or tokens.
