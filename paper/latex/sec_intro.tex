An LLM harness is the program of prompts, tools, and control flow that wraps
a frozen model. Automatically generating many such programs creates an
evaluation problem: what evidence shows that a new program provides a new
capability? Different source code may execute the same path; different paths
may produce the same answer; different answers on one run may reflect
execution randomness rather than a repeatable advantage.

We study this problem on BIRD text-to-SQL \citep{bird2021}. In a Phase-I
discovery population, 21 of 66 generated-candidate pairs had identical
correctness vectors (36 of 91 including \react{} and \bare{}), and
pairwise code similarity had no rank association with outcome
disagreement ($\rho=-0.01$). Trace inspection found three situations:
claimed mechanisms absent from control flow, mechanisms present but
untriggered, and broken multi-turn implementations. These observations
motivate an audit of the measurement process; they do not establish that
generated harnesses generally collapse.

A subsequent pre-specified factorial study crossed strategy forcing and
conformance gating over six builders and three seeds. Its primary
evaluation used 1{,}169 questions from nine databases outside protocol
development, with one disclosed 18-item smoke-test exception. Under this
revised setting, free ungated generation already produced 8.6\,pp
observed oracle headroom; the Phase-I pattern did not recur, but changes
in generation, judge, data, and execution prevent attribution to any one
protocol component without a controlled bridge. The gate also changes
the admissible strategy space: two allowed single-call prompt
transformations cannot pass its mechanism check. We preserve that
experiment and report a versioned post-review reanalysis; the primary
contrast remains $\RevisionPrimaryDelta$\,pp (95\% CI
$\RevisionPrimaryCI$), which neither demonstrates improvement nor
establishes equivalence.

Our contribution at this stage is a measurement method with
calibrated statistical guarantees, plus two empirical case studies:
\begin{enumerate}
\tightlist
\item \textbf{Code, trace, and outcome diversity differ.} Discovery
examples show why an inactive branch and a missing mechanism require
different tests.
\item \textbf{Admission and inference define the comparison.} A factorial
study and an explicit correction of its data and statistical chain show
which intervention was actually evaluated and what its estimate supports.
\item \textbf{Observed oracle value is not stable or usable value.}
Repeat instability motivates separate reporting of oracle accuracy,
best-fixed accuracy, repeated-run advantages, and realized selector
performance.
\item \textbf{A size-calibrated diagnostic separates the claim classes.}
The complementarity decision cross-fits a frozen selector against the
best \emph{fixed} member and calibrates against a same-code clone
randomization null; simulations show the prior plug-in false-supports
on pure noise while the calibrated rule stays nominal and reports power
honestly by repeat count (\S\ref{sec:diagnostics}).
\end{enumerate}
The decisive question is whether distinct generated programs deliver a
frozen-selection gain over spending the same executions on one fixed
program, with advantages that survive independent repeats. A fresh
pre-registered MATH-500 collection supplies exactly this control
(\S\ref{sec:wp1r}). On this panel the gain is within the declared
$1$\,pp margin but, owing to coverage gaps, formally
\textsc{Insufficient Evidence} rather than a strong negative: the
clone-calibrated difference is $\WpVthreeD$\,pp (CI $\WpVthreeDCI$). Stable
complementarity therefore remains an estimand to be tested with more
repeats on other panels and domains, not a discovered property of this
one.

This boundary suggests a sharper object for population construction:
useful routing depends on stable task--harness interaction after execution
noise is separated --- the estimand our diagnostic is built to test.
