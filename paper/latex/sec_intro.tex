Should a fixed inference budget buy more programs or more executions of
one program? Automated agent design makes the first option increasingly
accessible: a builder writes LLM harnesses that arrange prompts, tools,
and control flow around a frozen solver
\citep{adas2024,tthe2026,selfharness2026}. A population of such programs
promises a division of labor, with different members serving different
tasks. The practical value of that promise depends on whether the
programs offer advantages that persist and can guide a choice.

The first sign of such an advantage is often additional coverage:
one program answers a question that another misses. Yet the same pattern
can arise from a single program. Two independent executions with success
probability $0.5$ cover a task with probability $0.75$. Pass@$k$ captures
this opportunity; self-consistency and sampling-and-voting ensembles
turn multiple attempts into an aggregated answer
\citep{chen2021codex,selfconsistency2022,moreagents2024}. An oracle that
sees all outcomes can therefore reward a population even when every
member has identical code. With only a few repeats, selecting the
largest estimated success rate can continue to reward lucky outcomes.

We study how this initial coverage signal develops into useful task
specialization. Three questions guide the analysis. Does the population
supply additional correct answers? Do its task-wise advantages recur in
a fresh execution? Can a rule choose a helpful program before execution,
and how does the resulting coverage compare with additional attempts
from a fixed program? Each question moves one step closer to a decision
about where to spend the inference budget.

Our central experiment changes program identity while holding the
number of members and repeats fixed. Eight generated harnesses plus
\bare{} are compared with nine byte-identical baseline slots, each run
three times on the same $\RcvPairedN$ MATH-500 tasks. Two repeats identify
task-wise choices; the third tests whether those choices transfer.
A selector frozen on development data then makes choices from question
features, while an execution-matched replay compares the answers
available at increasing budgets. The full population of $35$ generated
programs supplies the initial single-run coverage picture. Supporting
BIRD traces follow program differences into execution, revealing how
implementation and activation shape the behavior behind a score.

The results tell a consistent story. Identical programs yield positive
oracle headroom. Generated programs do have substantially more
repeatable score patterns, but persistent losses span
$\RcvLossTasks$ tasks, while persistent wins occur on one. This structure
maps weaknesses more clearly than useful task advantages: the
clone-adjusted held-out gain is $\RcvD$ pp, the frozen selector gains
$\RcvPolicyGain$ pp, and both arms reach
$\RcvReplayFullCoverage\%$ oracle coverage at 27 executions.

The study contributes a same-code reference for interpreting oracle
headroom, a repeated-execution analysis that separates persistent losses
from wins, and a joint view of frozen selection and execution-matched
coverage. Together, they make the population's value concrete: which
answers it adds, which differences persist, and which advantages inform
a usable choice.
