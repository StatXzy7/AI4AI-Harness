\begin{abstract}
Do generated LLM harnesses add task-specific abilities, or merely more
opportunities to produce correct answers? We separate coverage,
repeatability, and usable selection using same-code controls on
$\RcvPairedN$ fully observed MATH-500 tasks. Eight generated programs
plus a baseline are compared with nine byte-identical baseline slots,
each executed three times. The clones produce $\RcvClonePlugin$
percentage points of repeat-averaged oracle headroom despite zero
program specialization. Generated programs show much stronger
residual score-pattern correlation ($\RcvRealRho$ versus $\RcvCloneRho$),
but persistent losses to the baseline span $\RcvLossTasks$ tasks,
whereas persistent wins occur on only one. The frozen feature selector
gains zero; at 27 executions, both arms reach
$\RcvReplayFullCoverage\%$ oracle coverage. Held-out-repeat evidence
leaves stable complementarity unresolved. Supporting BIRD traces
distinguish mechanisms that are absent, untriggered, or executed but
broken. Thus, in the tested panel, strong repeatability yields neither
a gain for the frozen selector nor greater full-budget oracle coverage
than additional executions of the same program.
\end{abstract}

\subsection*{Ethics Statement}
All experiments use public BIRD and MATH-500 benchmark data and generated
program artifacts; no human participants, private records, or personally
identifiable information are collected.  Model calls are reported as part of
the experimental provenance, and the paper does not release credentials or
provider responses that would expose private data.  The main ethical risk is
overstating what one-run oracle gains imply for deployed routing; we therefore
report the unresolved selection and cost claims explicitly as
\textsc{Insufficient Evidence}.
