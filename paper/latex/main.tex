\begin{abstract}
When a population of LLM harnesses solves more tasks than its best
member, does the gain reflect program specialization or simply more
attempts? We distinguish coverage observed after execution, task
advantages that persist across repeats, and gains realized by a
selector. The decisive control runs identical copies of one program:
on MATH-500, nine single-call slots produce a positive repeat-averaged
oracle estimate of $\WpVthreeClonePlugin$\,\%, despite having no program
specialization. Eight generated harnesses plus the baseline yield
$\WpVthreePlugin$\,\% on their own complete-task set. Holding out repeats
and calibrating against clones gives a mean gain of $\WpVthreeD$\,\%
on $\WpVthreeNPaired$ paired tasks; incomplete coverage and low sensitivity
leave stable specialization unresolved. A separately frozen selector
chooses the baseline on every task and gains $\WpPiZDiff$\,\%.
These results identify an attribution problem: positive population
coverage does not establish task specialization, and coverage
opportunities require a separate test of usable selection. We provide
a controlled evaluation that keeps these judgments distinct. Gains
are absolute probability differences expressed as \%.
\end{abstract}

\subsection*{Ethics Statement}
All experiments use public BIRD and MATH-500 benchmark data and generated
program artifacts; no human participants, private records, or personally
identifiable information are collected.  Model calls are reported as part of
the experimental provenance, and the paper does not release credentials or
provider responses that would expose private data.  The main ethical risk is
overstating what one-run oracle gains imply for deployed routing; we therefore
report the unresolved selection and cost claims explicitly as
\textsc{Insufficient Evidence}.

\subsection*{Reproducibility Statement}
The manuscript specifies the populations, task splits, execution
conditions, scoring rules, statistical procedures, and sensitivity
analyses used for the reported results. The appendices provide
additional protocol details, calibration results, and worked examples.
Experimental code is not provided to reviewers; the methodological
and empirical claims are presented within the manuscript.
